# Quick Reference · Management Communication Phrases

[⬅ Back to README](../README.md) · [Quick Reference Index](README.md)

Ready-to-use phrasing for the moments that matter most in a face-to-face interview with management — briefing an incident, pushing back respectfully, admitting a mistake, and closing strong.

## Briefing structure — the six-part shape

1. What happened (one sentence)
2. What is affected
3. What we have done
4. What we don't know yet
5. What we need from you, if anything
6. When you'll hear from us next (a specific time)

## Ready phrases for a live incident briefing

> "Here's what's confirmed, and here's what's still under investigation — I won't guess at the parts I'm not sure of yet."

> "The immediate risk is [X]. We've already contained it by [Y]. What we're still working on is [Z]."

> "I'll have a further update for you by [specific time], regardless of whether anything has changed by then."

> "I don't yet know how they got in, and I'm not going to guess — I'll tell you as soon as I have evidence."

## Pushing back on authority, respectfully

> "I understand the operational impact, and I want to get this resolved as fast as you do. That decision isn't mine to reverse alone — I'm escalating it to [incident manager] right now so it gets the right level of decision."

> "I hear the urgency. Skipping that step creates a real risk though — let me show you the fastest way to do this that still keeps us compliant."

> "I can see both sides of this — here's the security risk of waiting, and here's the operational cost of acting now. I think this needs the two of you talking directly rather than me relaying between you."

## Owning a mistake

> "I need to tell you about something I did — I [blocked X / made this decision], and it caused [Y]. I've already [reverted it / fixed it]. Here's what I'm changing so it doesn't happen again."

## Explaining risk acceptance in plain terms

> "Here's the specific thing that could go wrong, here's how likely I think it is, and here's what it would actually cost us if it did. That's your call to make, not mine — but I want you to make it with the full picture."

## Explaining technical things to non-technical people

> "Think of it like [everyday physical analogy]." — then check comprehension: "Does that match what you were picturing, or should I explain it differently?"

## Declining to discuss something confidential

> "I can't go into the details of that — but what I can tell you is [the one thing they actually need]."

## Closing lines that land well

> "I don't think trust is something I can just claim — it has to be demonstrated through consistent behaviour: reporting my own mistakes honestly, following procedure even when it's inconvenient, and escalating things correctly instead of guessing."

> "To me, integrity in this role means doing the same right thing whether or not anyone is watching."

## What never to say

| Avoid | Say instead |
|---|---|
| "I would investigate further." | Name the exact fields, logs, or checks you would run. |
| "It's fine, don't worry about it." | State the real risk honestly, in plain terms. |
| "Security policy says no." | Explain the actual risk in business language. |
| "I'm very trustworthy / honest." | Point to a specific demonstrated behaviour instead. |
| "That's not my job." | "That's outside my role, but here's what I can do, and here's who owns the rest." |
